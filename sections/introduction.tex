\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A dance movement often begins before its most visible accent: weight shifts
prepare a step, and the body transitions toward a pose before reaching it.
For a humanoid dancing to music as it arrives, the next movement must be chosen
with incomplete knowledge of the sound it will accompany. Waiting for a musical
event before reacting limits the opportunity to prepare; using the actual future
recording changes the task into offline generation. Streaming also prevents
revising movement already committed to the output. Anticipating the music and
continuing the body are therefore coupled aspects of causal dance generation.

Music provides a distinctive conditioning signal: it does not prescribe a unique
action sequence. Multiple dances can suit the same soundtrack, but rhythm,
accents, and dynamic variation should guide when and how movements unfold.
Producing different trajectories for different songs is not enough; those
differences should express musical correspondence while retaining coordinated,
varied motion. For a humanoid, this relationship must accommodate the robot's
body and remain perceptible after execution. The objective is thus musical
suitability expressed through the robot's actual movement, not reproduction
of a single target choreography.

Music-to-dance models have made substantial progress in choreography and
expressive human motion~\cite{li2021aist,tseng2023edge,li2023finedance,li2025soulnet}.
DiscoForcing~\cite{ji2026discoforcing} connects causal audio-driven motion to
humanoid execution through motion conversion and tracking, while
RoboPerform~\cite{li2026roboperform} incorporates audio-conditioned expression
into a teacher--student control framework. We investigate a complementary
design: musical motion is planned directly in the robot's native space and
delivered as an explicit reference to an independently trained whole-body
controller. Retargeting is performed during data preparation rather than
online. Beyond removing a conversion step, this design makes the organization
of robot motion and its continuation explicit modeling problems.

Two coupled modeling problems arise in this setting. First, a robot-native
representation must organize whole-body structure while retaining local
articulation. Discrete--continuous (D+C) motion models provide a useful basis for this
purpose~\cite{wang2026dcmotion}, but combining a codebook with a residual does not
by itself specify which kinematic properties each component should preserve.
Second, each committed prefix determines both the latent history and the
reference state from which the next segment begins. Under Teacher Forcing (TF), these inputs
come from recorded motion; at deployment, they depend on the model's own
samples. Generated-history training addresses related
mismatches~\cite{bengio2015scheduled,chen2024diffusionforcing,xiao2025motionstreamer},
but a boundary-conditioned D+C generator must also maintain correspondence
between its committed components and the state induced by their decoded movement.

We address these problems with \method{}, a framework for anticipatory
music-conditioned generation of robot-native dance. A frozen causal music model
predicts upcoming music states from the audio received so far~\cite{magenta2026mrt2}.
These forecasts provide prospective context without access to future waveform
samples. They condition both a discrete structural planner and a
structure-conditioned residual diffusion model through frame-wise
modulation~\cite{perez2018film}, connecting musical anticipation to body
organization and complementary articulation.

Our kinematics-guided D+C representation couples this generator to explicit
reconstruction objectives. Discrete-only reconstruction is supervised on
designated root, joint, and endpoint quantities, while code embeddings combined
with the residual reconstruct the complete motion through a shared
boundary-conditioned decoder.
Structural supervision under residual corruption further encourages the
designated organization to persist when detail varies. The representation
therefore learns complementary roles through robot-kinematic supervision,
rather than relying on quantization alone to separate structure and detail.

To train continuation, we introduce \cof{}. A first pass samples structure
and detail, commits their shared prefix, and reconstructs the next boundary
state from the decoded reference motion. A second pass learns continuation
under this generated history--state pair. The training context follows the
same reference-update operation used during streaming inference, exposing the
continuation model to the coupled consequences of its structural and detail
predictions.

The generator repeatedly plans, commits a prefix, and updates its reference
context (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). A reference bridge connects this stream to a
fixed SONIC whole-body controller~\cite{luo2026sonic}. The generator maintains
reference-derived context, whereas SONIC uses measured robot state for physical
feedback. Since delivery and tracking can change timing and motion dynamics,
our evaluation protocol distinguishes music--motion matching, music adaptation,
and dance quality at the generated-reference and executed-trajectory layers.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Kinematics-guided robot-native D+C representation and generation.}
Complementary reconstruction objectives explicitly supervise structural
properties in discrete codes and combine them with continuous detail through
shared decoding and coupled music-conditioned generation.
\item \textbf{Commit Forcing for streaming continuation.}
Joint commitment of sampled structure and detail, together with reconstruction
of the resulting reference boundary state, enables continuation training
under a generated history--state transition.
\item \textbf{A causal music-to-robot framework with layered evaluation.}
Forecast-conditioned robot-native generation is connected to fixed whole-body
control through a reference interface. The evaluation protocol distinguishes
music matching, conditional adaptation, and motion quality before and after
execution.
\end{itemize}

FineDance experiments show that forecast-conditioned models improve
music--motion matching over separately trained no-music controls under both
mixed-start and continuation-start training, with the same direction across
three training seeds. We also examine the effect of motion-history corruption
on dance quality and present a selected 60-second generation.
