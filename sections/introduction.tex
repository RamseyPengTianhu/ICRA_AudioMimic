\section{Introduction}
A dance movement often begins before its most visible accent: weight shifts prepare a step, and the body transitions toward a pose before reaching it. For a system generating dance as music arrives, the next movement must therefore be chosen with incomplete knowledge of the music it will accompany. Waiting for that music sacrifices the opportunity to prepare; using the actual future recording changes the task into offline generation. The central problem is how to obtain useful musical lookahead while respecting a causal audio stream.

Music-to-dance models have made substantial progress in choreography and expressive motion~\cite{li2021aist,siyao2022bailando,tseng2023edge,li2024lodge}. Streaming introduces an additional constraint: the model cannot revise movement already committed to its output. A new musical condition must be translated into a continuation of that movement, rather than a fresh, independent dance segment. In robot-native motion, the committed prefix also determines the pose and velocity at the next segment boundary. Anticipating the music and continuing the body are thus coupled aspects of the same generation problem.

We address this problem with \method{}, a framework for \emph{anticipatory music conditioning in streaming dance generation}. A frozen generative music model extrapolates from the audio heard so far. Its forecast states condition upcoming motion at their corresponding physical times. This gives the dance generator a hypothesis about the music ahead without exposing it to future waveform samples. The generator repeatedly plans a short horizon, commits a prefix, and replans as more music arrives (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). A forecast can therefore guide preparation without fixing the entire future dance.

The usefulness of this interface depends on how motion is organized. Discrete codes provide a compact vocabulary of movement, while continuous residuals can retain variation within that vocabulary. A generic combination, however, leaves their division of labor unspecified. Our \emph{kinematics-guided structure--detail decoupling} couples the representation to its training: discrete reconstruction is supervised on root, leg, and waist quantities together with torso, wrist, and sole geometry; full reconstruction is supervised on the complete motion. These views prioritize body organization while retaining complementary articulation. The geometry constraint also connects the roles: wrist placement belongs to the structural view even though arm-joint articulation belongs to the complementary native-coordinate view. A structural planner and a structure-conditioned detail generator reunite through a shared decoder.

A second challenge arises when the model continues from its own output. Teacher Forcing (TF) trains on recorded histories and their recorded boundary states. During generation, both instead depend on the preceding sampled motion. Replacing the history alone leaves the starting state disconnected from the movement that produced it. \cof{} constructs the complete generated transition: it samples structure and detail, commits their shared prefix, and reconstructs the boundary state from the decoded motion. The next plan is then trained under this history--state pair. This connects the training context to the same commitment operation used at inference.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Anticipatory music conditioning} supplies forecast states inferred from heard audio to both motion generators, enabling musical lookahead within a causal streaming interface.
\item \textbf{Kinematics-guided structure--detail decoupling and training} assign complementary motion views to discrete structure and continuous detail, with coupled generation and shared decoding.
\item \textbf{Commit Forcing} trains the next plan using jointly committed generated motion and its reconstructed boundary state, addressing the context mismatch created by successive generation decisions.
\end{itemize}

FineDance experiments establish music-condition use under both mixed-start and continuation-start training: forecast conditioning improves music--motion matching over separately trained no-music controls, with the same direction across all three training seeds. We also examine how history corruption affects the motion distribution and show a selected 60-second generation. The matched tests of forecast versus heard-music conditioning, representation alternatives, and CoF versus TF are specified separately from these completed results.
