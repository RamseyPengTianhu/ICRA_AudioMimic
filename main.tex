\begin{abstract}
Generating humanoid dance from live music is not only a sequence-generation problem: the system must operate causally, meet a fixed replanning budget, preserve support over long horizons, and continue from states caused by its own previous outputs. We present \method, a robot-native streaming framework that repeatedly plans short Unitree G1 motion segments and commits only an executable prefix before replanning. The motion representation separates a discrete structural token from a continuous residual, while a state-conditioned decoder reconstructs G1 root, joint, and contact references. To reduce the training--deployment mismatch induced by repeated self-generated context, we introduce \emph{Commit Forcing}, which performs a second training forward pass from an atomically committed discrete--continuous prefix and its reconstructed physical boundary state. Across 39 strict-causal 60-s rollouts from 13 held-out motions, Commit Forcing reduces mean motion error by 69.6\%, tail error by 92.3\%, and no-support rate by 66.8\% relative to a matched one-forward control. Its 135.1-ms replanning p95 remains below the 266.7-ms commit interval. These results establish the motion-only streaming backbone. The full system further introduces causal anticipatory beat conditioning and execution-state feedback through a whole-body tracker; their audio-conditioned and physical-robot evaluations are ongoing and are not included in the current quantitative claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ================================================================
Music-to-dance methods commonly generate an entire motion sequence using access to the complete audio track. This setting is useful for choreography but mismatched to a live humanoid: future audio has not yet arrived, generation must finish before the next action deadline, and the next plan should reflect what the robot actually executed. Directly concatenating independently generated windows creates seams, whereas training only from ground-truth histories exposes the model to contexts that will not exist during deployment.

The problem is especially pronounced for humanoids. A visually plausible character animation may still contain unsupported flight, foot sliding, joint discontinuities, or root motion that a physical controller cannot track. Moreover, beat response is anticipatory: a robot must begin shifting support and preparing a gesture before a musical accent arrives. Detecting a beat at its onset and reacting afterward is insufficient once perception, generation, communication, and actuation delays are included.

We therefore formulate online humanoid dance as \emph{strict-causal receding-horizon robot motion generation}. At each replanning cycle, the generator receives only committed motion history, the current physical boundary state, and causal conditions. It predicts a short future horizon, commits a prefix, discards the unused suffix, and replans, as summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:streaming_architecture}. The present paper draft first establishes a stable motion-only backbone and defines the two remaining components required for the final system: causal beat anticipation and feedback from physics-based execution.

The intended paper contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A strict-causal, robot-native receding-horizon formulation for live humanoid dance: the model predicts a future horizon, atomically commits a discrete--continuous prefix, updates motion history and physical boundary state from that prefix, and replans without future audio or motion.
    \item Commit Forcing, an execution-consistent extension of generated-context training in which the second forward pass starts from the same atomically committed D+C prefix and reconstructed physical state used at deployment. This is the central contribution currently supported by 60-s experiments.
    \item \todo{To validate} BeatAhead, an uncertainty-aware causal beat predictor and prepare--hit--release conditioner that gives a physical humanoid lead time to prepare for future accents, rather than reacting only after a beat is observed.
    \item \todo{To validate} execution-state closure, which replans from the state actually produced by the tracker/robot and quantifies how tracking error propagates back into online generation.
\end{itemize}
The discrete--continuous latent and the whole-body tracker are enabling components rather than standalone novelty: related work already studies hybrid motion representations and direct audio-conditioned humanoid policies. Our novelty claim therefore rests on causal long-horizon planning, commit-consistent training, anticipatory beat use, and execution closure. We retain uncompleted components in the draft because they determine the final ICRA system, but mark their experiments explicitly.

\section{Related Work}
% ================================================================
\subsection{Music-Conditioned Dance Generation}
EDGE~\cite{tseng2023edge} combines a Transformer diffusion model with Jukebox features to generate editable, arbitrarily long dance sequences and introduces contact consistency training and a Physical Foot Contact metric. MEGADance~\cite{yang2025megadance} instead targets genre-aware offline choreography using FSQ-based motion quantization, kinematic--dynamic reconstruction constraints, and a genre-routed MoE with a Mamba--Transformer backbone. AIST++~\cite{li2021aist} and FineDance~\cite{li2023finedance} provide paired music--motion data used by these methods. These works establish strong offline music-to-human-motion baselines, but neither defines a deployment-faithful loop in which only arrived audio is available and each new plan is conditioned on the robot state caused by previously committed outputs.

\subsection{Streaming Motion Generation}
MotionStreamer~\cite{xiao2025motionstreamer} introduced causal motion latents and Two-Forward training to reduce autoregressive exposure bias. DiscoForcing~\cite{ji2026discoforcing} studies real-time audio-responsive character control using a causal music encoder and diffusion forcing. We build on the general idea of training from generated histories but focus on the commit semantics required by a receding-horizon robot planner: discrete structure and continuous detail must be committed together, and the next forward pass must use a physical state reconstructed from that same committed action prefix.

\subsection{Hybrid Motion Representations and Humanoid Tracking}
Purely discrete motion tokenizers provide structural abstraction but lose continuous detail, whereas continuous diffusion preserves detail without an explicit symbolic layout. DC-Motion~\cite{wang2026dcmotion} factorizes human motion into discrete structural tokens and continuous residuals. We adapt this principle to causal G1 motion planning with contacts and a state-conditioned decoder. For physical execution, general humanoid trackers such as SONIC~\cite{luo2025sonic} provide an interface between kinematic references and whole-body control. In our final system, the tracker is not only an output module: its executed state closes the next planning cycle.

\subsection{Direct Audio-Conditioned Humanoid Control}
RoboPerform~\cite{li2026roboperform} is the closest robot-control work. It aligns audio and motion latents with an InfoNCE adaptor, trains a residual mixture-of-experts teacher, and distills a two-step diffusion student that directly outputs G1 joint targets. It demonstrates Isaac Gym training, MuJoCo transfer, and physical Unitree G1 execution with low per-action latency. Consequently, direct audio-to-action mapping, retargeting-free execution, and real-robot dancing cannot by themselves be our novelty. Our problem is complementary: RoboPerform trains and evaluates aligned 10-s audio--motion clips and does not formulate strict-causal streaming, repeated horizon commit, exposure to self-generated planning history, explicit future-beat uncertainty, or replanning from measured execution state. \method{} retains an explicit reference-planning layer to model long-horizon choreographic structure, then closes that planner around the tracker.

\section{Problem Formulation}
% ================================================================
Let $a_{\leq t}$ denote audio that has arrived by time $t$, $\hat{x}_{<t}$ the robot motion prefix already committed by the planner, and $s_t$ the physical state at the current plan boundary. The objective is to sample a future robot reference horizon
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_{t:t+H-1} \sim p_\theta\!\left(x_{t:t+H-1}\mid
    \hat{x}_{<t},s_t,a_{\leq t}\right),
    \label{eq:causal_objective}
\end{equation}
without reading future audio or future ground-truth motion. Only the first $C<H$ steps are committed. The remaining plan suffix is discarded, the boundary state is updated, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:causal_objective} is evaluated again.

The current implementation uses the following fixed contract:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $K=64$ committed latent steps (approximately 4.27~s) as motion history;
    \item a 66-D physical boundary state $s_t$ containing root height, velocity and acceleration, 29 joint positions, and 29 joint velocities;
    \item an $H=8$ latent-step plan, decoded to 16 motion frames;
    \item a $C=4$ latent-step commit, corresponding to eight frames or approximately 266.7~ms.
\end{itemize}
This action chunking allows each committed action to consider a short future while keeping the replanning interval bounded.

\section{Robot-Native Streaming Generation}
% ================================================================
\subsection{Discrete--Continuous Motion Latent}
Each causal motion step is represented by a discrete structural prototype $q_t$ and a continuous residual $r_t$:
\begin{equation}
    z_t = e(q_t) + r_t,
    \qquad q_t\in\{1,\ldots,512\},\quad r_t\in\mathbb{R}^{256},
    \label{eq:dc_latent}
\end{equation}
where $e(\cdot)$ is a learned codebook. The discrete component is intended to capture coarse motion stage and support transition, while the residual preserves joint detail and local dynamics not represented by the selected code.

A state-conditioned robot-native decoder maps the combined latent and boundary state to G1 motion and contact:
\begin{equation}
    (\hat{x}_{t:t+1},\hat{c}_{t:t+1}) =
    D_\psi\!\left(z_t,s_t\right),
    \label{eq:decoder}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{x}$ contains a 34-D G1 motion representation and $\hat{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ contains left/right foot-contact logits. Figure~\ref{fig:dc_latent} illustrates the decomposition and shared decoder. The generator therefore operates directly in robot motion space at inference time; human-to-G1 retargeting is used only when constructing the training set.

A single 512-entry codebook represents coarse structure, while the continuous quantization residual preserves joint detail, velocity profile, contact timing, and local articulation. The shared robot-native decoder reconstructs either the full D+C motion or the structure-only counterpart for analysis.}

\subsection{Factorized Future Generation}
The structural branch is a 12-layer causal Transformer with width 768 and 12 attention heads. It autoregressively predicts the eight future $q$ tokens. Conditioned on the sampled structure, committed history, and $s_t$, a continuous diffusion branch jointly generates the corresponding eight residuals. The continuous model uses a temporal context network followed by nine AdaLN denoising blocks and is trained with a cosine-noise, $x_0$-prediction objective:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}=
    \mathbb{E}_{r_0,\epsilon,n}
    \left[\left\|f_\theta(r_n,n,q,h,s_t)-r_0\right\|_2^2\right].
    \label{eq:diffusion_loss}
\end{equation}
Training uses 1000 diffusion levels; current inference uses DDIM with NFE=10. The discrete and continuous outputs form one inseparable plan and must use the same commit boundary.

\subsection{Receding-Horizon Commit}
For each cycle, the model produces
\begin{equation}
    P_t=\{(q_{t+i},r_{t+i})\}_{i=0}^{H-1}.
\end{equation}
The decoder causally reconstructs the corresponding 16-frame G1 reference. Only
\begin{equation}
    P_t^{\mathrm{commit}}=
    \{(q_{t+i},r_{t+i})\}_{i=0}^{C-1}
    \label{eq:atomic_commit}
\end{equation}
is appended to history. Both latent components are committed atomically; mixing a discrete prefix from one plan with residuals from another would change the represented motion. The new physical boundary state is computed from the decoded committed prefix. During final deployment, this idealized reconstruction will be replaced or augmented by measured tracker/robot state.

\subsection{Commit Forcing}
One-forward training predicts the next horizon from ground-truth history, although inference conditions on self-generated commits. Commit Forcing makes this mismatch explicit (Fig.~\ref{fig:commit_forcing}). Given ground-truth context $h_t$ and state $s_t$, the first forward pass samples
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{P}_t\sim p_\theta(P_t\mid h_t,s_t).
\end{equation}
We stop gradients through this sample, atomically commit its first $C$ steps, causally decode them, and construct generated context
\begin{equation}
    (\tilde{h}_{t+C},\tilde{s}_{t+C})
    =\mathcal{U}(h_t,s_t,\tilde{P}_t^{\mathrm{commit}}),
    \label{eq:generated_context}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{U}$ is the same history/state update used at deployment. A second forward pass predicts the subsequent target from this generated context:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CF}}=
    \mathcal{L}_{q}\!\left(p_\theta(q_{t+C:t+C+H}\mid
    \tilde{h}_{t+C},\tilde{s}_{t+C})\right)
    +\lambda_r\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{res}}.
    \label{eq:commit_forcing}
\end{equation}
Residual rebasing keeps the full target latent unchanged when the generated discrete prototype differs from its ground-truth counterpart: the residual target is recomputed relative to the generated codebook vector. This prevents the two latent components from representing inconsistent target motions.

    \caption{\textbf{Commit Forcing.} Forward~1 generates an H8 D+C plan, stops gradients, decodes it, and atomically commits C4. The committed prefix updates K64 and reconstructs S66. Forward~2 learns the next H8 plan from this generated deployment context. Residual rebasing preserves the target full latent when the generated discrete code differs from the ground-truth code.}

\section{Causal Anticipatory Music Conditioning}
% ================================================================
\subsection{Why Current-Beat Conditioning Is Insufficient}
The current validated results are motion-only. Earlier offline ablations showed that richer or higher-dimensional beat features can change dance behavior, but beat alignment, motion distribution, and physical quality do not improve together. A live robot additionally requires lead time: support transfer must begin before the target accent. We therefore treat beat anticipation, rather than feature dimensionality alone, as the next research question.

\subsection{BeatAhead}
\todo{Implementation and quantitative evaluation ongoing.}
A causal audio encoder reads only $a_{\leq t}$ and predicts $M$ hypotheses for upcoming rhythmic events:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{B}_t=
    \left\{(\Delta t_m,p_m,d_m,u_m,\sigma_m)\right\}_{m=1}^{M},
    \label{eq:beat_hypotheses}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_m$ is time-to-beat, $p_m$ the hypothesis probability, $d_m$ the downbeat probability, $u_m$ the accent strength, and $\sigma_m$ timing uncertainty. We currently plan $M=3$ to retain alternatives under syncopation or tempo changes.

Each hypothesis is converted to an asymmetric prepare--hit--release field $\Phi_m(t)$. Preparation grows before a candidate beat, the hit component peaks near the event, and release decays afterward. A lead-time policy suppresses actions that can no longer be safely prepared: full preparation is allowed above 150~ms, shortened gestures are used between 50 and 150~ms, and late accents below 50~ms are not chased aggressively.

The structural token generator receives beat-slot cross-attention, while the continuous residual branch receives a FiLM/AdaLN modulation. This separation lets predicted rhythmic structure influence phase transitions while continuous conditioning controls local timing and intensity.

\section{Physics-Based Execution Closure}
% ================================================================
\todo{SONIC simulation and physical-G1 results to be added.}
The generated G1 reference is passed to a universal whole-body motion tracker such as SONIC~\cite{luo2025sonic}. A purely feed-forward interface would leave the next generator state equal to the planned reference even when the controller lags or deviates. We instead define execution closure:
\begin{equation}
    s_{t+C}^{\mathrm{next}} =
    \begin{cases}
    s_{t+C}^{\mathrm{ref}}, & \text{reference-state baseline},\\
    s_{t+C}^{\mathrm{exec}}, & \text{execution-aware planning},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:execution_closure}
\end{equation}
where $s^{\mathrm{exec}}$ is estimated from simulated or physical robot state. The final online loop is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    a_{\leq t} &\rightarrow \text{BeatAhead}
    \rightarrow \method \rightarrow \text{commit}\\
    &\rightarrow \text{tracker}
    \rightarrow s^{\mathrm{exec}}_{t+C}
    \rightarrow \method.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The comparison in Eq.~\eqref{eq:execution_closure} isolates whether closing the planner around actual execution prevents long-horizon drift under tracking error and disturbances.

\section{Experiments}
% ================================================================
\subsection{Motion-Only Protocol}
We evaluate 13 held-out source motions with three sampling seeds, producing 39 strict-causal 60-s closed-loop rollouts per trained method. All methods use the same $K64/H8/C4/S66$ streaming contract. Two Commit-Forcing models are trained from independent random initializations to test repeatability.

We report mean and 95th-percentile motion MSE, joint jerk, root drift, no-support rate, and p95 replanning latency. Motion MSE measures deviation from the corresponding evaluation motion under the current aligned protocol; because dance continuation is multimodal, we do not interpret MSE alone as perceptual quality. No-support rate is the fraction of frames in which neither foot has reliable near-ground support.

\subsection{Long-Horizon Results}
Table~\ref{tab:motion_results} reports the current validated results. Commit Forcing substantially reduces both average and tail error relative to the matched one-forward control. The improvement is reproduced by an independently trained model. The one-forward model obtains lower joint jerk, but its much larger motion error, drift, and no-support rate show that this reflects loss of activity in failed long rollouts rather than superior motion quality.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
% ================================================================
The current evidence supports stable robot-reference planning, not yet autonomous physical dancing. First, the motion-only evaluation does not demonstrate music responsiveness. Second, $S66$ is reconstructed from the committed reference rather than measured execution, so current rollouts close the generator around its outputs but not around robot dynamics. Third, aligned motion MSE is useful for diagnosing rollout divergence but is not a complete quality metric for multimodal dance. Fourth, sampled discrete planning is currently the validated decoding route; alternative decoding strategies may have different long-horizon behavior. Finally, the 13-motion evaluation should be expanded with more held-out motions, music genres, training seeds, and confidence intervals.

These limitations motivate rather than invalidate the next stages. BeatAhead tests whether causal future-beat hypotheses create measurable preparation before an accent. Execution closure tests whether the same planner remains stable when the boundary state is produced by a tracker and physical dynamics. Together they convert the validated streaming backbone into the intended real-time music-to-robot system.

\section{Conclusion}
% ================================================================
We presented the current stage of \method, a robot-native receding-horizon generator for online humanoid dance. A discrete--continuous representation combines structural planning with continuous detail, and Commit Forcing trains the generator from the same committed latent and physical-state context it encounters during deployment. Current 60-s strict-causal experiments show large and repeatable improvements over one-forward training while meeting the reference-planning latency budget. The remaining work is deliberately scoped: causal anticipatory beat conditioning, attribution ablations, and execution-state closure through simulation and a physical G1. These additions will determine whether the stable motion generator becomes a complete real-time music-responsive humanoid system.
